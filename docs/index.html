    How it was built

    1. 1Collect

      Five sources, fetched automatically: the US Water Quality Portal, GEMStat and Eionet bathing waters (via Open Water Quality), Open-Meteo rainfall, and Natural Earth regions. Every file is kept exactly as received, with a checksum.

    2. 2Choose sites

      Up to 60 sites per area and water type, picked with a fixed random seed from sites with at least 24 samples over 3 years. Same seed, same sites, every time.

    3. 3Check

      Automatic checks at every layer: raw files, staged data and the final database tables. A critical failure stops the run.

    4. 4Tidy

      All sources become one table with one set of names and units (CFU/100 mL). Every row we drop is counted, with a reason.

    5. 5Add rain

      Each sample gets the rainfall of the previous two and three days, and is flagged if it exceeds the EPA limit.

    6. 6Score

      A year is poor if more than 10% of sampling days exceed the limit. A site is a persistent hotspot if it is poor in at least 3 of its last 5 years. Regions are then ranked.

    7. 7Store and schedule

      Results are loaded into PostgreSQL and the whole flow runs with Apache Airflow in Docker, so another machine gets the same answer.

    What to keep in mind

    • A sample, not every site. Results describe the sites we chose, not every water body.
    • Different periods. US data runs to 2025, Eionet ends in 2021 and GEMStat effectively in 2018, so each area’s “recent years” differ.
    • Different networks. River stations, bathing sites and agency networks are not directly comparable.
    • Laboratory upper limits. Many GEMStat values sit at reporting maximums (for example 24,196 MPN/100 mL), which means “at least this much”. Severity is understated there.
    • Gaps in open data. Asia and Africa had too little open fecal-indicator data to analyse. That reflects data sharing, not necessarily cleaner water.
    • Rainfall comes from gridded reanalysis, not gauges at the sites.
